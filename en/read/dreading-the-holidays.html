Dreading the holidays? Why the season feels heavy and how to get through it

The first holiday ad shows up and your stomach drops. Everyone around you is talking about plans and recipes, and you are quietly counting the days until it is over. Then comes the guilt: what kind of person dreads the most wonderful time of the year?

Plenty of people, as it turns out. The holidays pack family, money, memories and expectations into a few short weeks. Dreading that is not a character flaw. It is usually a sign that something in the season costs you more than it gives back.

Signs the season is weighing on you

  • You feel tense or flat whenever holiday plans come up
  • You avoid replying to the family group chat
  • Sleep gets worse as the dates get closer
  • You keep rehearsing conversations you have not had yet
  • Spending money on gifts makes you anxious, not excited
  • You feel lonelier in a crowded room than you do alone

A few of these are common. If you notice most of them, or the heaviness lingers well after January, it is worth taking seriously. This article is general information, not a diagnosis.

Where the dread usually comes from

  • Family tension. Old roles come back the moment you walk through the door.
  • Questions you dread: work, dating, kids, weight, money.
  • Grief. An empty chair is louder at the holidays than any other time.
  • Money pressure, especially when everyone else seems to be spending freely.
  • Being alone while the whole world looks like it is gathering.
  • Shorter, darker days that already pull your mood down.
  • Pressure to feel happy, which makes any other feeling seem wrong.

Most people are carrying more than one of these at once. Naming which one is loudest for you is the first useful step, because each one needs a different kind of care.

Decide a few things ahead of time

Much of the dread comes from uncertainty. You can take some of it away by settling small decisions before the day arrives.

  • Choose how long you will stay, and plan your exit before you arrive.
  • Prepare one short answer for the question you dread most, then change the subject.
  • Set a gift budget and say it out loud to someone. It is easier to keep that way.
  • Book one quiet hour for yourself each day: a walk, a drive, a closed door.
  • Pick one person you can text when it gets hard.

None of this is rude. Limits are what let you show up at all.

If you will be on your own

Spending the holidays alone can feel heavier because of how loudly the season advertises togetherness. It can help to give the day a shape rather than leaving it empty. Cook something you actually like, plan a long walk, call one person, or volunteer for a few hours. Some people start their own small tradition and come to look forward to it. Your day does not have to look like the ads to count.

If you are grieving

The first holidays after a loss are often the hardest. You do not have to keep every tradition, and you do not have to pretend. Some families set a place, light a candle or share one story about the person who is missing. Others change everything that year. Either is fine. Grief does not follow a calendar, and it is allowed to show up at the table.

When it is more than holiday stress

If low mood, hopelessness or exhaustion stay with you for two weeks or more, or you find yourself thinking you would be better off not here, please reach out to a doctor or a crisis line today. You do not need to wait until the holidays are over to ask for help.

Getting it off your chest

Sometimes the hardest part is that you cannot say any of this out loud, not to family and not with everyone so busy being merry. Writing it down where nobody knows it is you, without having to make it sound nice, can take some of the pressure off before the season begins.

If there is something you have been carrying alone, writing it down without your name attached is one way to set it down.
